# Winner 12: "Confidently Wrong: Measuring and Mitigating Calibration Risks in LLMs for African Languages"

Source: `submission/reference/winners/globalsouth-africa-confidently-wrong.pdf` (12 pp, read in full). Global South AI Safety Hackathon, June 2026, regional winner (Africa). Authors: Okunowo, Koto, Misker (AIMS South Africa) "With Apart Research".
Judges' reviews: https://apartresearch.com/project/confidently-wrong-measuring-and-mitigating-calibration-risks-in-llms-for-african-languages-hrve (4 unnamed reviewers; the page shows no per-criterion scores I could read; reviews 3-4 are partly collapsed, so the quotes below come from a summarising fetch and some may be paraphrased).

## 1. Core claim and title
- **Core claim, one sentence:** open-weight LLMs are less accurate *and* more overconfident in African languages than in English (higher ECE), and cheap per-language temperature scaling brings calibration error back to near-English levels without retraining (Abstract p1; Table 1 p3; Sec 6 p4).
- **Title style:** a hook phrase that states the finding ("Confidently Wrong"), then a descriptive subtitle ("Measuring and Mitigating Calibration Risks in LLMs for African Languages"). The subtitle also signals the two-part contribution: diagnosis plus fix.

## 2. Research question and impact story
- RQ (Sec 1, p1): "We investigate whether models become increasingly overconfident in African languages, quantify the extent of this misalignment between confidence and correctness, and evaluate practical strategies for mitigating it."
- Impact chain (Sec 1, p1): LLMs are used for high-stakes information (health, legal, civic) → users "cannot easily verify an answer" → confident wrong answers count as "trusted misinformation" → equity angle: "the populations least served by current models are most exposed to confident misinformation."
- Gap claim (Sec 2, p2): "To our knowledge, calibration in African languages, compared across models and paired with mitigation strategies, has not been systematically studied."
- Three numbered contributions (p1-2): (1) an open, reproducible calibration audit; (2) evidence of degradation from English to African languages; (3) mitigations plus a deployment framework.

## 3. Method
- **Models (Sec 3.1; App A Table 2 p5):** 4 open-weight models chosen "to span size, multilinguality, and origin": Qwen3-4B-Instruct, Gemma-4-12B (it), Aya-Expanse-8B, AfroLlama-V1 (7B, LLaMA-2 base). All run in bf16, sdpa, 2048-token context, greedy decoding, on one A100 40GB.
- **Data (Sec 3.2):** Uhura-TruthfulQA (English plus 6 African languages; variable number of options) and AfriMMLU (18 languages; 4 options). The two datasets are "analyse[d] separately and never average[d] across them."
- **n:** never stated. No item counts per language or per dataset appear anywhere. Table 4 shows only a "representative subset" of 8 of the 18 AfriMMLU languages.
- **Scoring (Sec 3.3; App B p5):** cloze scoring, i.e. length-normalised log-prob of each option's answer text, softmaxed over the options; confidence = max prob. They explicitly avoid letter-logit scoring because it "silently collapses instruction-tuned models, with Gemma-4 returning 'A' for 75% of items and scoring below chance". This is an instrument-validity check: they show the naive method fails and say why they chose the other one.
- **Metrics (Sec 3.4; App B p6):** ECE (15 equal-width bins), overconfidence (mean confidence minus accuracy), Brier, error-detection AUROC, AUARC. All are defined formally.
- **Baselines/controls:** English is the within-model reference condition (en vs afr). Temperature scaling's baseline is T=1. Few-shot is compared with zero-shot. **There is no positive control** (for example a known-calibrated model or synthetic confidence), and there is no chance-level or shuffled-label reference for ECE.
- **Held-out sets:** temperature T* is fitted on a "seeded 50% calibration split" and ECE is reported "on the held-out half" (App B p6; App D p6). Few-shot exemplars are "held out from evaluation" (App C-D p6). The T grid is {0.5,...,5.0} (App D). Note that T*=5.0, the grid edge, is chosen for 7 of 14 language-model cells in Table 5 (p8), which suggests the grid was too narrow. The paper does not comment on this.
- **Seeds:** only a fixed seed 42 for the bootstrap and a "seeded" calibration split. Decoding is deterministic, so there is no run-to-run variance. Split-to-split variance of T* is not reported.
- **Pre-registration:** none.

## 4. Statistics
- **CIs:** 95% bootstrap CIs, 2,000 draws, stratified by language, seed 42, percentile method, for ECE(en), ECE(afr), Acc(en) and Acc(afr) (App G p7; Table 7 p8; Fig 7 p12). They state that the macro-averaged estimates "can differ by up to about 0.004 from the pooled values in Table 1." They also explain why the English CIs are wider (a single stratum).
- **Tests:** no hypothesis tests and no p-values. Inference is made visually from disjoint CIs (Fig 7 caption: "For the general-purpose models the African interval lies entirely to the right of ... the English interval; the intervals are disjoint").
- **Effect sizes:** raw differences in ECE and accuracy only. No standardised effect sizes, and no CI on the en-afr *difference* itself.
- **Multiple comparisons:** none, even though there are 4 models x 2 datasets x up to 18 languages.
- **Variance in the main text:** a single sentence pointing to Appendix G (p3). The main Table 1 has point estimates only.
- **Where the claims run ahead of the data (useful for us):** AfroLlama's ECE is *lower* in African languages (Uhura 0.13 vs 0.18; AfriMMLU 0.20 vs 0.22, Table 1), and Gemma's Uhura accuracy is higher in African languages (0.34 vs 0.33). Yet the Conclusion says the result holds "uniformly across the models and two tasks" (p4). The Fig 3 caption says ECE drops "to about 0.03 for all models" while Table 1 has Gemma/AfriMMLU at 0.07. Table 1's ECE(TS) is computed on the held-out half and ECE(afr) on all items, so the before/after comparison mixes item sets (Table 5's pooled values are the like-for-like ones). The judges did not penalise any of this.

## 5. Honesty devices
- **Failed predictions / nulls reported:** few-shot "backfires on Gemma" (Sec 4 item 3; Table 6: ECE 0.251 → 0.332). Abstention "helps less, because uncertainty is a weak error-ranker (AUROC about 0.55)". The letter-logit collapse is also disclosed.
- **Counter-intuitive finding stated plainly:** "Bigger is not safer": Gemma-4-12B, the largest model, is the worst calibrated (ECE up to 0.34).
- **Limitations:** one paragraph inside Sec 5 "Discussion and Limitations" (p4). It covers near-chance accuracy ("we document systematic overconfidence rather than catastrophic confident-wrongness"), "absolute ECE is moderate except for Gemma", the weak AUROC that limits abstention, and the scope (4 open models, 2 benchmarks, no reasoning or frontier closed models). It also flags out-of-distribution languages for AfroLlama (Amharic, N. Sotho; App A, Table 3 dagger).
- **Threats to validity:** there is no formal section. The data-scarcity threat (TS needs labelled in-language data) is *not* raised; a judge raised it instead.
- **Dual-use / ethics section:** **absent.** (Our sprint requires one, so we cannot copy this.)
- **AI-use disclosure:** yes (Sec 6, p4): "We used Claude to help build and debug the evaluation pipeline code. All results were produced by our code and independently verified. Idea was conceptualized and plan was designed by the team."

## 6. Presentation
- **Length:** 12 PDF pages in total. The **main body is only about 4 pages** (Abstract to Conclusion, pp1-4), references are on p5, and Appendices A-G fill pp5-12.
- **Sections:** Abstract; 1 Introduction (with numbered contributions); 2 Related Work (one paragraph); 3 Methods (3.1 Models, 3.2 Datasets, 3.3 Scoring, 3.4 Metrics, 3.5 Interventions); 4 Results; 5 Discussion and Limitations (bold run-in heads: Implications for African AI safety / Limitations / Future work); 6 Conclusion (+ "Code and data", "LLM usage"); References (6); Appendix A Models, B Metric definitions, C Prompts (boxed templates), D Interventions, E Deployment framework (the heading is empty and its figure floats to p9, a layout glitch), F Additional results, G Bootstrap CIs.
- **Figures (7):** Fig 1 ECE heatmaps, model x language (2 panels, main text); Fig 2 deployment-framework flowchart (TikZ); Fig 3 ECE before vs after TS bar charts; Fig 4 accuracy radar plots; Fig 5 overconfidence heatmap + risk-coverage curve; Fig 6 reliability diagrams for 4 models, en vs afr; Fig 7 forest plot of bootstrap CIs. **Tables (7):** Table 1 main summary; Table 2 models; Tables 3-4 per-language Acc/ECE (bold where ECE exceeds the English baseline); Table 5 per-language T* and ECE before/after; Table 6 zero- vs 5-shot; Table 7 bootstrap CIs.
- **Money figure:** Table 1 (p3). In one 8-row table it gives Acc(en), Acc(afr), ECE(en), ECE(afr), the bolded ECE(TS) and AUARC, so problem and fix appear side by side. Its caption states the takeaway: "Models are accurate and calibrated in English but lose both in African languages, and recalibration restores calibration." Fig 1, the heatmap, is the visual money figure. The most convincing statistical figure (Fig 7, disjoint CIs) is buried on the last page.
- **Abstract structure** (about 170 words): context → risk → setup (models and benchmarks in bold, metric) → main finding → mitigation result → implication. First two sentences: "Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in African languages, yet little is known about whether their confidence remains reliable. This creates a safety risk: models may become less accurate while remaining highly confident, increasing the likelihood of trusted misinformation."
- **Bolded takeaways:** Results is a numbered list of 4 items, each opening with a bold claim sentence: "**Calibration degrades from English to African languages.**", "**The accuracy collapse is a knowledge story.**", "**Mitigations are unequal.**", "**Bigger is not safer.**" Model and benchmark names are bolded in the abstract, and ECE(TS) values are bolded in Table 1.
- **Captions carry the claim** (Fig 1, 3, 4, 6, 7 and Table 1 captions all end with the takeaway sentence).

## 7. Reproducibility
- Code release: "All code, predictions, figures, and tables are in the repository" (GitHub link, Sec 6 p4). The datasets are named by their HF ids (masakhane/uhura-truthfulqa, masakhane/afrimmlu).
- Full prompt templates (App C), HF model IDs, hardware and decoding settings (App A), metric formulas (App B), T grid (App D), bootstrap procedure and seed (App G).
- No verify scripts, frozen protocol or pre-registration are mentioned. "Independently verified" (Sec 6) is asserted, but the paper does not say how.

## 8. Judges' praise and criticism (apartresearch.com project page)
- **Praise:** "a significant safety risk on the continent (confident but incorrect answers in African languages)" and novelty across models (R1); "the temperature scaling result is immediately actionable for practitioners" and the execution is rigorous (R2); "This is a strong paper that identifies a specific failure mode with broad implications", it goes beyond diagnosis by testing 3 interventions, and it has bootstrap CIs, a held-out calibration split, multiple metrics and "excellent" presentation (R3); "well-executed and addresses an important problem" (R4).
- **Criticism:** "the authors could benefit from explaining in less technical language" (R1). The deployment framework is only proposed and not validated empirically (R2). "some of the measured overconfidence reflects a model with almost no signal" because AfriMMLU is near chance (0.29 vs 0.25); TS needs labelled in-language data, which is scarce; they suggest a data-efficiency sensitivity analysis and that the authors "move the evaluation closer to frontier models" (R3). "frontier closed models and reasoning models explicitly out of scope"; "Accuracy on these benchmarks sits near chance for most African languages"; real-world harm is not directly measured (R4).
- **Pattern:** the judges rewarded a clear safety framing, a fix that practitioners can act on, and visible statistical care (CIs, held-out split). Their criticism focused on whether the signal is above the floor (near-chance accuracy), external validity (frontier models), and claims that were proposed but not tested (the framework).

## 9. Transferable lessons for our multi-agent collusion paper
1. **Put the finding in the title and pair diagnosis with a cheap fix.** "Confidently Wrong" plus a mitigation that works on the existing models drove both the "impact" and the "actionable" praise. Our analogue: a hook title that names the collusion finding (warned vs not-warned, security-officer detection), plus at least one countermeasure that we actually tested, such as the messages-only security officer or team-only reward, reported with before/after numbers in the same table.
2. **Make one main table hold the whole contrast, with a caption that states the takeaway.** Table 1 sets condition A next to condition B, then adds the post-intervention column. For us: rows = model/config, columns = collusion rate (not warned), collusion rate (warned), detection rate, detection after countermeasure. Keep a 4-page core and push per-seed and per-condition breakdowns to the appendix.
3. **Report results as a numbered list of bold claims, and include the nulls.** "Mitigations are unequal" and "few-shot backfires on Gemma" made the paper look honest rather than weaker. We should state plainly any condition where warnings or the officer did not help, and any counter-intuitive scaling result (their "Bigger is not safer").
4. **Show the signal clears the floor, with a positive control and CIs on the difference.** Two reviewers attacked near-chance accuracy, and they had no positive control. We need (a) a positive control, e.g. scripted or explicitly instructed colluders that the detector must catch, plus an honest-only negative control for false positives; (b) bootstrap CIs over episodes/seeds clustered by run, with a CI on the warned-minus-unwarned *difference* and not just disjoint marginal CIs; (c) the exact n per cell, which this paper never gave.
5. **Pre-empt the standard judge critiques in Limitations and the required ethics appendix:** generality beyond the models tested (frontier/closed), real-world harm not measured directly, proposed vs validated components (only claim what we ran), data or cost requirements of the countermeasure, and plain-language summaries for non-technical readers (R1). Also include an AI-use disclosure line and a code/data link listing the exact results files. This paper had no dual-use section; our sprint requires one, so write it properly.
- Avoid their slips: overclaiming "uniformly across models" when one model reverses, caption numbers that disagree with the table, an empty appendix heading, and the key CI figure buried on the last page.
