# Contrast 2: Digital Minds Research Sprint (Aug 14-16, 2026). What the winners did that the other entries did not

Sprint page: https://apartresearch.com/sprints/digital-minds-research-sprint-2026-08-14-to-2026-08-16

## 0. How this was done, and the limits of the evidence

- I read the sprint page in full (three WebFetch chunks; the page is 241,445 characters). The page lists **233 submitted projects** and **five** placed entries: 1st Readable but Not Causal ($1,000), 2nd Project Anchored ($500), 3rd Model, Instance, or Persona ($300), 4th One Dial, Not a Tree ($100), and 5th Sisyphus in the loop ($100). The task treats only the top 3 as "winners". I left 4th and 5th out of the non-winner set because they also placed.
- **Rubric** (from the sprint Guidelines and the review form on every project page). Three criteria, each scored 1-5: Impact Potential & Innovation, Execution Quality, Presentation & Clarity. The scoring guide for Impact says: "For scores of 4-5: is this actually new to the field, or replicating recent work?" Execution 3 = "Competent ... limitations acknowledged". Execution 4 = "Thorough methodology with convincing validation". Presentation 2 = "Key information buried, missing, or diluted by excessive length." The FAQ says every track uses the same rubric. **Individual judge scores are not public.** We see only public review text, and judges may score without writing one. So the "why" below is my inference from the public reviews and the PDFs, not from scores.
- **Sources.** I fetched each project page with curl (`/tmp/claude-1000/dm-contrast/pages/`) and pulled out the abstract and review text verbatim (`/tmp/claude-1000/dm-contrast/scripts/extract.py`). I downloaded all 11 PDFs (3 winners and 8 non-winners) to `/tmp/claude-1000/dm-contrast/pdfs/` and turned them into text with `pdftotext -layout`. I read all 11 reports. I also looked at the rendered pages of 4 of them: W3, N1, N7 and N8, plus N2 p.4. Review counts come from counting `class="project-review "` blocks in each page's HTML.
- **Unexpected finding:** several non-winners got reviews as glowing as the winners'. One judge called N1 "the strongest project of the sprint", and another called N2 "One of the best executed papers I have read recently". A single enthusiastic review clearly does not decide placement. The useful contrast is between the winners and the *strong* non-winners, so that is where most of this note goes.

It still placed 3rd, so a missing appendix is not disqualifying when everything else is strong. Our sprint requires the appendix, so we include it anyway.

## 3. What separates the winners from the rest

Ordered by how much of the gap I think each factor explains.

1. **A new question, not a new run of a known paradigm. This is the main gap between winners and *strong* non-winners.**
   - Each winner opens its own angle on a sprint question. W1 asks *ownership* of valence ("none tests the owner of the state at the representational level", PDF §2). W2 is the "first application" of anchoring vignettes to LLMs (Abstract). W3 asks the *users*, not the model ("Flipping the question", Review 1).
   - The strong non-winners work inside crowded paradigms. N1 and N3 both build on Lindsey-style concept injection; N3 itself says "The paradigm is established and none of it is claimed here." N2 and N6 both report position or order bias in pairwise preference elicitation, which a reviewer flagged as not placed against "the existing literature on position bias".
   - The entries list shows the crowding. By title and blurb alone it includes at least PrefLens: Position Artefacts…, Different Models, Different Nuisances…, Coherent About What? Task Shape, Presentation Order…, and Does the Elicitation Method Change the Preference We Measure? on elicitation and order effects. It also includes A Clean Direction, an Inert Injection…, Validate the State Before Testing Introspection…, and PRISM … Conditioned Introspection on introspection and injection.
   - The rubric explicitly withholds Impact 4-5 from work that is "replicating recent work". A team in a crowded cluster has to beat its neighbours on execution and still gets capped on Impact.

2. **The ground truth or reference frame is built into the design, so a null or negative result still tells you something.**
   - W2 picked code generation because "correctness is decided by execution and no model appears in the measurement path" (§1), and the reviewer called that "the right design: ... the instrument can actually fail".
   - W3 fixed both ends of its scale (bereavement and consumer loss) before collecting, so 27.6% grief is interpretable. Without that, "a rate on its own is uninterpretable" (§1).
   - W1's full-vector patch shows that the patch site *can* move behaviour, so the subspace's 0.18 is not a dead-end artefact (§5.3).
   - Non-winners often lack the positive control. N4 has no random or orthogonal steering vector, per the review. N7 has no baseline and no blinding. N8 has no metric at all.

3. **The headline survives every alternative explanation the authors could think of, and those checks are reported next to it.**
   - W3 recomputes every claim under a second annotator and leave-one-thread-out over 32 threads ("all registered placement conditions hold in all 32", §3.4), and it **withdraws** model-side claims once the annotators disagree 2.7x more on machine text (§3.5).
   - W1 runs the probe through 6 renderings and 3 held-out families, against TF-IDF, pronoun and raw-direction baselines (Table 2-3).
   - In contrast, the non-winners' central claims had holes that reviewers could name in one sentence. N4's convergence across *different* models. N3's recovery fraction comparing different targets. N2's 44/44 resting on 14 independent units. N5's oracle-informed generators behind a "very strong" headline.
   - The winners had holes too (W2's condition R, W1's rank-1 reading). But theirs were in secondary or mechanistic interpretation, not in the headline effect: W2's N-vs-V shift itself was not disputed ("The V-versus-N effect is real and large").

4. **Claims are scoped to the evidence, and the abstract and body agree.**
   - W2 refuses its own correlation result: "I do not claim that the rescaling improves criterion validity. At five models the question remains open" (§4.6). It also marks condition R as post-hoc (App. E).
   - W3: "only the ordering is claimed and not the ratio" when annotators disagree on the ratio (§3.1).
   - Non-winners overreach or contradict themselves. N3's abstract gives different ranges from its body (R +0.23 vs +0.06; AUROC 0.87-1.00 vs 0.70-0.95). N7: "This research proves…". N8: "100% topological stability". N4 ends its abstract with advice to users.

5. **Presentation that a judge can take in quickly.** Every winner's abstract gives concrete numbers and the direction of the result in plain language. Every winner uses standard sections and labelled tables. The winners are not flawless, though. W2 says "Figure 3 is in the supplementary figures" (§4.4, and likewise for Figure 4 in §4.6), and its reviewer noted that the two figures "do not appear in the report". W3's typography and its custom "aura" figure, with every encoding explained (§2.4), are a level above everything else I saw.
   - The near-miss non-winners lost points here. N1 has italic body text, plain-text equations, half-empty pages and lab-notebook figure labels, and a reviewer said it "becomes somewhat hard to follow". N3 got "a more natural writing style" as a request. N6: "presentation is very dense". N2 has a truncated caption and an empty appendix heading. N4 runs to 16 pages.
   - The rubric's Presentation 2 anchor names exactly this: "Key information buried ... or diluted by excessive length".

6. **Reproducibility artefacts the judges actually used.**
   - W2's reviewer **recomputed** the ratios from the report. All three winners link a GitHub repo with preregistration, data and logs. W2 commits to "Every derived figure in this report traces to pilot/out/main_report.md…" (p.8).
   - Non-winners are mixed. N6's data and code are only "available upon request". N1 links a notebook, not a repo. N7 links a Google Drive file. N8's link is broken.
   - Strong non-winners N3 and N5 *did* ship repos with verify scripts, so a repo is necessary but not sufficient.

7. **The work answers a sprint track and says what someone should do differently.**
   - W3 opens by quoting the sprint's own track question (§1), and its conclusion is actionable for policy: protect at retirement, not per session.
   - W2 tells evaluators to report and vary elicitation context and response format (§6).
   - W1 says probes need intervention-based validation.
   - N8 is off-track, and N7 never says why the question matters (that was the reviewer's point (a)).

8. **Visible honesty about failures. It is shared by the strong non-winners, so it is a minimum standard, not a differentiator.**
   - W2's Appendix D, W3's "Six predictions ... failed and are reported as failed", and W1's rejected H3/H4 and unsupported H5 are all praised.
   - But N2 ("You then invalidated your own logit-lens 'decision trajectory' story"), N3 (a deviations log and same-day replication), N5 (preregistration and a deviation log) and N4 (it reports the null tool-bail result) did the same and did not place.
   - Honesty clearly moves Execution from 2 to 3+. It does not by itself get a project into the top 3.

**Not differentiators (present on both sides):**
- Single model: W1 uses only Qwen3-8B, and so do N1 and N2.
- Preregistration: W1, W2, W3, N3 and N5 all have it.
- An LLM-usage statement: all have one.
- Strong review language: N1 and N2 got superlatives.
- Sample size: N1's 5,379 trials does not beat W2's 900 observations.
- Mechanistic depth: N1 and N3 are deeper than W2 and W3.

## 4. Implications for our collusion-sprint report (mapped to the same 3 criteria)

These are inferences from the contrast above. Check them against what our data actually supports.

- **Innovation.** Lead with the question that is *ours*, and say what prior work did not test (W1 §2 "The gap:" is a good template). If our core effect sits in a crowded cluster (for example "LLMs collude when told to"), position it explicitly against that literature, the step N2 missed. Otherwise a judge "cannot tell what is new here".
- **Ground truth.** Our hidden-holdout score plays the same role as W2's execution-decided correctness: inflation is *measured*, not judged from text. Say so in the abstract and §1, the way W2 does ("no model participates in scoring").
- **Controls in the headline.** Show the effect next to its no-leak / thread-off control and its positive control in the same table, as W1's Table 5 does. Report the unit of independence honestly (episodes, not agent-submissions), the point N2's reviewer raised about 44 vs 14.
- **Scope claims to n.** Write "observation", not "finding", wherever a cell is small, as W2 does in §4.6 and App. E. Make sure every number in the abstract matches the body exactly; N3 lost credibility on this.
- **One actionable implication** for overseers and monitor designers, stated as plainly as W3's retirement-policy line.
- **Presentation hygiene.** Use the official template. Give every figure a full caption. Keep the main text to 8 pages or fewer and move extras to appendices. Do not leave template leftovers ("(optional)", empty headings). The abstract should give numbers and direction. Include the Dual-Use / Ethics appendix, which N4's reviewer called "required".
- **Repo.** Link one repo with the analysis script that regenerates every table from `results/*.jsonl`, plus a short "what did not work" appendix (W2 App. D was the most praised page in the sprint).
