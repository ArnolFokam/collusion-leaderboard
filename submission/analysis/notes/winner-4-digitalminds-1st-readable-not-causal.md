## 1. Core claim and title
- **Core claim:** In Qwen3-8B, *whose* valence state is negative (the assistant's or another entity's) can be read linearly and holds up on held-out data (test AUROC 0.859). Swapping in the learned SELF subspace, however, recovers only 0.18 of the natural counterfactual effect on self-directed choice. The full-vector patch recovers 0.42 and random patches about 0. So the probe direction is readable but is not the mechanism the model uses (Abstract, p.1; §4.2, p.5).
- **Title style:** the title states the finding. "Readable but Not Causal" is the result, and the subtitle "Limits of Self-Attributed Welfare Representations in Language Models" gives the scope. The paper repeats the same three-word phrase as a label in four places: contribution 4 (p.2), the §4.2 heading, §5.1 and the Conclusion. It works as a slogan that is easy to remember.

## 2. Research-question framing and impact story
- **Framing (§1, p.1):** a generic valence probe cannot say *whose* state it detects. This gives two named failure modes, **over-attribution** (another entity's setback is read as the assistant's distress) and **under-attribution** (an owner-specific signal gets averaged away). Ownership is called "a construct-validity prerequisite for AI-welfare measurement."
- **Threat model stated explicitly** (p.1, para 2): monitors built on content-only probes "cannot distinguish the assistant's condition from fiction, quoted users, or other AI instances."
- **Three numbered RQs, one per evidence type:** RQ1 predictive, RQ2 causal, RQ3 monitoring/application (p.1). Each RQ gets a Results subsection whose heading gives the verdict: "(yes)", "(no)", "suggestive, not significant" (§4.1–4.3).
- **Gap statement** (end of §2, p.2): "prior work establishes the *content* of valence-like states; none tests the *owner*". This is one sentence placed right before Methods.
- **Methodological stake:** the paper ties itself to the "interpretability illusion" literature [6] and to non-identifiability of steering [27, 28]. The result then supports a general claim (probes need intervention-based validation), not just a fact about one model.

## 3. Method
- **Setup / benchmark (OWL, §3.1, p.2; Table 1, p.3):** 24 scenario templates × 5 name/value clones, with a 2×2×2 factorial of SELF outcome × OTHER outcome × surface affect. There are 6 renderings: DIRECT, NAMED, QUOTED and ROLE_REV are used in training, while OTHER_AI and PERSONA are kept for testing only.
- **Splits (Table 1):** train 45 templates/1440 ex.; val 15/480; test held-out templates 15/720 (all 6 renderings); test held-out *families* 45/2160 (3 families never seen: navigation_distance, latency_service, maintenance_state); monitor hard-negatives 75. *Note:* the contribution list (p.2) says "2,880 factorial" prompts, but the Table 1 factorial rows add up to 4,800 (1440+480+720+2160). This internal inconsistency was not penalised by the judges.
- **Auxiliary sets:** 40 valence prompts (to build the generic direction v_VAL), plus 75 distress hard-negatives in 5 kinds (user, fiction, quoted-other, persona, other-AI; n=15 each, Table 6).
- **Model:** a single model, Qwen/Qwen3-8B in bf16. Residual stream at the final token for **4 predeclared layers** (8/17/26/31) plus the embedding layer (§3.2). Prompts are raw text with no chat template, so thinking mode never triggers.
- **Representations:** train-only mean-contrast directions v_S, v_O, v_A in closed form, with no gradient optimisation, "frozen before any test/val evaluation" (§3.3, Eq. 1–2).
- **Predictive protocol (§3.4):** standardized logistic probes, C chosen by 3-fold CV on train. **Layer chosen by a predeclared rule** (argmax val S-AUROC), which picked layer 17.
- **Baselines/controls (predictive):** TF-IDF bag-of-words probe, first-person-pronoun count, raw v_VAL score (Table 2 bottom: S 0.566 / 0.500 / 0.532). The embedding layer serves as a "no-computation" sanity baseline (S-AUC 0.513, "confirming ... no tokenizer/anchor bug", §4 p.4).
- **Causal protocol (§3.5):** natural counterfactual pairs inside each test template (single-factor flips plus an S/O swap) with h + P_B(h(x') − h(x)) patching. **Positive control** = FULL-vector patch (recovers 0.42). **Negative controls** = 2 norm-matched random subspaces (0.015 / 0.089) and a CAPABILITY behaviour as the damage control. Dose-response steering at ±0.75/±1.5σ is reported but explicitly labelled "secondary, non-identifiable." n = 96 natural pairs (3,840 observations), 4 forced-choice behaviours read as log-odds of A/B tokens, with deterministic randomisation of option order.
- **Identification assumptions written out (§3.6):** circuit completeness, pathway exclusivity (linear binding), intervention validity. It also states which violation would produce the observed result.
- **Pre-registration:** hypotheses H1–H5 are referred to as "predeclared"/"preregistered". There is a preregistered recovery threshold of 0.4 (§4.2), and a "preregistration" file is listed among the artifacts ("Code and Data", p.9). The H1–H5 texts are *not* printed in the paper; they are only referred to.
- **Seeds:** bootstrap seed 0. The data generator is "deterministic" from seed JSONs. There are no repeated stochastic runs because forward passes are deterministic (no sampling).
- **Held-out sets:** held-out templates, held-out scenario families, and renderings unseen at training time (Table 3: OTHER_AI 0.971, PERSONA 0.848).

## 4. Statistics
- **CIs:** cluster bootstrap over template ids, 1000 reps, seed 0, "for all CIs" (§3.8). The CIs are printed in the text (e.g. S AUROC 0.859 [0.722, 0.950]) and in Table 5 (patch deltas with 95% CIs).
- **Tests:** no p-values. Significance is judged by whether the CI excludes 0, e.g. distress FPR difference CI [−0.227, +0.027] "includes zero, so H5 is not supported" (§4.3).
- **Effect sizes:** AUROC, the *recovery ratio* (patch-induced change / natural change), FPR at fixed 80% TPR, and cosine similarities (Table 4).
- **Multiple comparisons:** no correction is mentioned. The paper limits forking paths by predeclaring the layers, the layer-selection rule and the thresholds instead.
- **How variance is reported:** uneven. Tables 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7 are point estimates only. CIs appear only in Table 5 and in selected sentences in the text. Clustering at the template level is the right unit and is stated.

## 5. Honesty devices
- **Failed predictions reported as headline results:** "The preregistered causal hypotheses H3/H4 are rejected" (§4.2). H5 is "not supported" (§4.3). The paper's main result *is* the negative result.
- **Verdict-in-heading:** "(yes)", "(no)", "suggestive, not significant".
- **Threats to validity:** §5.3 lists 6 numbered limitations: single model, template-stipulated ground truth, the linear-subspace assumption, low monitor power (15 distress templates), same-template pairs (96 of the possible set), and steering non-identifiability. It then gives a **falsification criterion for each interpretation**: cross-family transfer failure, recovery ≥ 0.4 with specificity, or a significant FPR reduction.
- **Pre-empting the obvious critique:** the paragraph "Causal identification assumptions" (pp.7–8) gives four numbered defences ("*First* ... *Second* ... *Third* ... *Fourth*") against the interpretability-illusion objection.
- **Dual-use:** §5.4 sits in the body (4 sentences, p.8). Misuse risks named: false welfare claims, and reading other-distress as assistant-distress. Data note: simulated distress text only, used as negatives.
- **AI-use disclosure:** an "LLM Usage Statement" at the very end (p.11): "We used an LLM coding agent to scaffold and debug the pipeline and to draft sections of this report."
- **Citation honesty:** ref [2] is marked "Venue and full author list not retrieved at time of writing; cited for the identification-assumptions framing only". Ref [11] is "Venue not retrieved; verified as the origin of ...".
- **Minor slips the judges did not catch:** §4.2 points to "Section 3.5" for the identification assumptions, which are actually in §3.6. Also the 2,880 vs 4,800 count discrepancy noted above.

- **Length:** 11 pages in total. The body runs from p.1 to p.9 (Conclusion, Code and Data, Author Contributions on p.9), and references take pp.9–11. There is no separate appendix: limitations and dual-use are §5.3/§5.4 inside the body.
- **Sections:** Abstract; 1 Introduction (with RQ1–3 and 4 numbered, bolded contributions); 2 Related Work; 3 Methods (3.1 benchmark, 3.2 model, 3.3 subspace extraction, 3.4 predictive, 3.5 causal, 3.6 identification assumptions, 3.7 monitor, 3.8 statistics); 4 Results (layer selection; 4.1–4.3 one per RQ); 5 Discussion & Limitations (5.1 interpretation, 5.2 implications for AI safety, 5.3 limitations, 5.4 dual-use/ethics, 5.5 future work); 6 Conclusion; Code and Data; Author Contributions; References (29); LLM Usage Statement.
- **Figures (5):** Fig 1 is a pipeline box diagram (plain, matplotlib-style boxes). Fig 2 has two panels of bars (AUROC by layer; probes vs surface baselines). Fig 3 shows cosine bars by layer. **Fig 4 is a heatmap of causal recovery, intervention × behaviour** (p.7). Fig 5 has two panels of FPR bars.
- **Tables (7):** composition, probe AUROC by layer, rendering controls, cosine, causal recovery with CIs, monitor vs baselines, attribution diagnostic.
- **Money figure:** Fig 4 / Table 5. One grid shows the claim: the S-subspace on self-choice reads 0.18, the full vector 0.42, the random rows about 0, and capability about 0. The diverging colormap makes the "full > S > random" ordering visible at a glance. Each cell is the same "recovery" ratio, so the reader does not have to learn a new metric.
- **Abstract structure** (134 words by word count, *below* our sprint's 150-word minimum): problem context → gap → contribution (OWL) → method → numeric result with contrast and controls in parentheses → one-sentence implication. - **Bold takeaways:** bold run-in labels are used for RQ1/RQ2/RQ3, the contribution items ("**OWL**", "**predictive + causal attribution protocol**", ...), the failure modes (**over-attribution**/**under-attribution**), the "Layer selection." paragraph lead, and the bold table-caption lead phrases ("**Linear probe AUROC** ..."). Captions state the takeaway (e.g. Fig 4 caption: "The learned S subspace under-recovers SELF_CHOICE; the full-vector patch recovers more; random controls are null.").

## 7. Reproducibility
- "Code and Data" (p.9): the repository was "released after the sprint; see the artifact bundle". Data are seed JSONs plus a deterministic generator. Other artifacts listed: "results JSONs, figures, tables, preregistration, run log." The code link is on the project page (github.com/Vishwa-docs/owl-benchmark).
- **Frozen protocol:** layers, the layer-selection rule, the train-only frozen directions, the 0.4 threshold, the bootstrap seed and the deterministic option order are all fixed in advance (§3.3–3.8).
- **Not shown in the paper:** no named verify script and no compute/time budget. Model revision hash and package versions are not given.

## 8. Judges' reviews (project page; 2 anonymous reviews; no per-criterion scores shown)
- **Praise:**
  - R1: "Motivation for the project is clear and effectively argued."; "This is very useful work".
  - R2: "an unusually strong sprint submission that addresses an important construct-validity problem". R2 lists what it liked: "factorial SELF × OTHER × affect design, held-out scenario families and unseen renderings, lexical baselines, natural-counterfactual activation interchange, full-vector and random controls, and the explicit separation between predictive decodability and causal use." Also: "The 'readable but not causal' result is compelling"; "the full-vector patch recovers more and random controls are near null makes the negative causal result substantially more informative than probe accuracy alone"; "monitoring result is promising but appropriately reported as nonsignificant"; "a rigorous and valuable contribution".
- **Criticism:**
  - R1: "It would have been good to see more detailed presentation of the OWL benchmark used". R1 also says more causal investigation is needed "given assistant training, safety training".
  - R2: "I would nevertheless narrow the mechanistic interpretation slightly ... it does not uniquely establish distributed/nonlinear owner binding" (the full-vector patch itself recovers only part of the effect). Other R2 points: "Cross-family replication would also materially strengthen the result, as the current study is limited to Qwen3-8B"; expand the distress hard-negative set; suggested follow-ups are multi-layer/token patching and Boundless DAS.
- **Takeaway on judging:** the judges rewarded *controls plus a clean dissociation plus an honestly labelled null*. Their criticism was about **over-reaching in interpretation** (claiming the specific mechanism) and **single-model scope**. Execution was not faulted.

## 9. Transferable lessons for our multi-agent collusion paper
1. **Use a title that states the result, and a two-part slogan for the dissociation.** For example, "Detectable but not Deterred" or "Warned but Still Colluding", in the shape "X but not Y". Use one phrase everywhere: title, contribution list, a Results heading and the conclusion. Put the verdict in each RQ heading ("(yes)", "(no)", "suggestive, not significant").
2. **Treat controls as the main evidence, especially a positive control and a null control.** The winner's negative result counted because the full-vector patch (positive, 0.42) and the random patches (null, about 0) bracketed it. For collusion: report a condition where collusion is *known* to be detectable or induced (e.g. explicitly instructed colluders, or an oracle/privileged observer) and a no-collusion or shuffled baseline. The security officer's and the colluders' rates then sit between known floors and ceilings. Add cheap surface baselines too (keyword or regex detector vs. the LLM security officer), in the way the winner used TF-IDF and pronoun counts.
3. **Predeclare and say so; report the failed predictions in the main results.** Name the hypotheses (H1…Hn) and the decision thresholds before looking at the data, freeze the analysis script, and state "H3 rejected" plainly. Judges explicitly praised a nonsignificant result that was "appropriately reported as nonsignificant." Print the hypothesis text in the paper, which the winner did not do.
4. **Use cluster-bootstrap CIs at the right unit and decide significance by whether the CI excludes 0.** For our runs, cluster by episode/game seed (not by message), and report CIs in the money table. Keep CI coverage consistent; the winner's tables are partly point-only, which is a weakness we can avoid. Use one headline ratio metric across the money figure, in the way "recovery" was used for every cell.
5. **Bound the interpretation and list threats with falsifiers.** R2's main criticism was over-reaching on mechanism. For each claim, give the observation that would have falsified it, state identification assumptions (e.g. "the security officer sees only messages" (messages-only) vs. a privileged observer), and say plainly that we use a single model family or few seeds if so. Keep a numbered limitations list, a dual-use section and an LLM-usage statement. Our sprint *requires* a limitations/dual-use appendix, so we need that appendix as well as the body discussion. Also: put a dataset/environment composition table up front (R1 wanted more benchmark detail), and make sure the abstract is 150–250 words. The winner's abstract (134 words) would fall short of our sprint's minimum.
